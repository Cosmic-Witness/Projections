It is beneficial to first consider some basic linear algebra. Vectors can be defined in many different ways, and the definition you get depends on who you ask. A physicist will tell you that a vector is a mathematical object that has both a magnitude and direction; a computer scientist will say that it’s an array of elements; and a mathematician, being a mathematician, will give the most abstruse answer: that vectors are elements of vector spaces that can be added together and scaled by numbers. An example of a vector would be

v = 37.

This vector is in ℝ2, in two-dimensional space. To visualise this vector, a Cartesian plane with the traditional x and y axes would be used, with the first element corresponding to the vector’s position on the x-axis and the second element corresponding to the vector’s position on the y-axis. The point would be identified, and an arrow with its tail at the origin (0, 0) would be drawn.

It is worth noting, though, that our decision to define the first element as the x-axis position and the second as the y-axis position is completely arbitrary. These two could be swapped in our rule, and it would still be a valid way of representing our vector. We see that the nature of the “space” these vectors live in depends on how we choose to define it. This should be kept in mind. We should consider what exactly is going on when we choose to identify our vector’s position in this way. Recall that every vector can be written as a linear combination of other vectors; therefore, our vector v is also

v = 30 + 07.

Multiplying a vector by a number would “scale it”, meaning that it would stretch, squish or flip it in the vector space. The number that scales is fittingly called the scalar. For example, multiplying the vector (3, 0)T by the scalar 2 would stretch it to the vector (6, 0)T, doubling its size. If that’s the case, then what stops each of these component vectors from being the result of such a scaling? Nothing! The vector (3, 0)T, one of those that are combined to get vector v, can be thought of as the result of scaling the vector (1, 0)T by 3. This vector, (1, 0)T, is a “fundamental” vector that is scaled by all real numbers to produce all vectors along the x-axis. The same is true for (0, 1)T and the y-axis. These two fundamental vectors are called the basis vectors,

x̂ = 10 ŷ = 01.

So our vector v is really just the result of scaling x̂ by 3 and ŷ by 7 and adding the two together. In this way, we project a given vector along a basis vector. An n-dimensional space has n basis vectors, one for each of the dimensions. We say that for an n-dimensional space ℝn,

v = ∑i=1n aiêi,

a weighted sum of basis vectors.

Now, another way of interpreting the vector v, and the one most relevant to our problem, is how much each component of the vector (the x-component or the y-component) contributes to the overall vector (in this case, in terms of size). We can do this by getting the dot product of our vector v with our x̂ basis vector, and with our ŷ basis vector.

v · x̂ = 37 · 10 = 3, v · ŷ = 37 · 01 = 7.

The result of v · x̂ will give us a measure of how much of the vector is in the x-axis, and the result of v · ŷ in the y-axis (along the y-axis). In these operations, the basis vectors can be regarded as probes along their respective axes, searching for the strength of the vector along them. If the result of this probing for, say, x̂ were zero, it would mean that the vector has no strength along the x-direction. It is orthogonal to the basis vector. If we were to treat our vector as a signal, which it very well is, we have measured how much of the signal lies along each basis direction and know where most of the signal’s energy is concentrated: the y-axis in this case. So, by thinking about how the vector is distributed in its 2-dimensional vector space, we can obtain a lot of insight into the components that make it up. Therefore, for a unit basis vector ê, the scalar ⟨v, ê⟩ measures precisely how much of v lies along ê. This is the component of the signal’s energy concentrated in that direction.

To make the ideas underpinning this concrete, especially what exactly basis vectors are, we should think about what it takes for us to have a vector space with properly defined notions of geometry (lengths, angles, orthogonality). A vector space is merely a set of objects closed under addition and scalar multiplication, with these operations obeying a short list of natural rules (associativity, distributivity and the like). That is it. It doesn’t need to have geometry, and yet, as we have seen, geometry is an important feature to attach to these spaces. The question then is how this is done. Well, it is accomplished via the inner product function. The inner product of two vectors (often denoted ⟨a, b⟩) is a scalar, and this scalar allows us to formally define geometric notions. A vector space with this inner product function is elevated to an inner product space.

In the finite-dimensional inner product space that is Euclidean space, this function is more commonly called the dot product. This is defined as

a · b = ∑k=1n akbk.

It isn’t that hard to see how the dot product introduces geometry into a vector space, as it is used to determine the angles between vectors and hence establish orthogonality. Now basis vectors are just used as sort of reference vectors to build a coordinate system. It is convenient for the basis vectors of ℝ2, x̂ and ŷ, to be defined as they were above. Any other set of vectors could have worked, as long as they spanned the entire space and were linearly independent (such that every vector is a unique combination of them), but we use these to keep things simple (more on that soon). It is along these basis vectors that we project our vector v and compute the dot product (inner product) as was already shown.

A nonzero result for a dot product computation between a basis vector and the vector we wish to analyse indicates that a portion of that vector can be found along that basis vector. A zero result, however, means that the vector is completely absent along that basis vector. This clean reading holds precisely because each probe is orthogonal to the directions it is not meant to measure, so no other component contaminates the result; orthogonal probes do not share any information and aren’t entangled with each other in any way. It is very important that our basis vectors are orthogonal. Two vectors are orthogonal if and only if

⟨a, b⟩ = 0.

If they were not, then that value of 3 for the vector v along x̂ would also be shared with ŷ. It would be much more difficult to truly isolate the vector’s contribution along x. The basis vectors we chose also have the nice additional property of having a unit norm (which saves us the hassle of normalising projections to maintain consistency). They are therefore orthonormal (orthogonal with unit norm). This will not always be the case.

So, if this vector v were our signal and we wanted to know the strengths of the vector along the x and y directions, we would simply design “x” and “y” vector probes orthogonal to each other, get the inner product of each of the probes with the vector, which would neatly extract the strengths along these directions. Easy peasy!

Signals are usually the products of some phenomena through time, and these phenomena can be modelled by functions (that assign a value at every instant of time). So when we decide to take measurements of some signal-generating physical process, like a heartbeat, we are essentially sampling a function that describes the temporal behaviour of that process and storing its value at finitely many instants in a list. That list is a vector. Even more interestingly, functions themselves obey the same algebraic rules as vectors, as a sum of two functions f and g is defined pointwise as

(f + g)(t) = f(t) + g(t),

and scaling by a constant behaves identically. In this sense, functions are infinite-dimensional vectors.

We already established that functions live in an infinite-dimensional space. If we could project our signal into this space, along basis vectors that hold information about frequency, then we could find the coordinates of this signal, what frequencies it’s composed of and how strongly these frequencies contribute to the signal. The problem is that the operations we have used so far and the basis vectors only work for the finite-dimensional Euclidean space. We need to first extend this space into an infinite-dimensional one. Before we start looking for appropriate basis vectors, we first need to turn the discrete dot product into a continuous analogue, such that we can operate on functions instead of just finite arrays. This key change is precisely what will enable the extension to a proper function space.

The continuous version of discrete summation is integration. Therefore, the dot product becomes

⟨f, g⟩ = ∫ f(t)g(t) dt,

which is the inner product for vector spaces defined in infinite-dimensional space. The Euclidean inner product is a finite sampling of “pointwise correlation”. In contrast, this function-space inner product is the limit of that correlation as the sampling becomes infinitely fine (think of Riemann sums). This definition is, however, incomplete. The integral operation needs limits. This choice of limits, as we’ll see later, is vital to the definition of the space that we seek to construct. Changes in this interval alter the very geometry of the inner product space (the lengths, angles and even which vectors are orthogonal to one another).

Not every function can have an inner product defined over an interval. Consider the inner product of a function with itself,

⟨f, f⟩ = π∫−π f(t)2 dt.

Squaring the function before integrating in this way has some nice physical interpretations. In signal processing theory, this squared function yields a quantity that often corresponds to the energy of a signal. This makes the squared norm a natural measure of signal strength. If this integral diverges, the function has infinite length, and no sensible geometry can be built around it (as it would imply infinite energy in our case). So we filter out such functions and admit exactly the functions of finite energy, those satisfying

π∫−π |f(t)|2 dt < ∞.

This family of functions, over the interval [−π, π], is called L2([−π, π]): the square-integrable functions on that interval. This infinite-dimensional space, with its notions of lengths and angles supplied by the integral inner product function, is called a Hilbert space, after the legendary mathematician David Hilbert. (Strictly speaking, the name also requires the space to be complete, meaning that limits of well-behaved sequences of functions do not escape the space.) It is into this space that we will project our signal. Of course, the computer will never manipulate the infinite-dimensional function directly. Instead, it will manipulate a finite collection of samples that approximates it.

We now need to choose appropriate basis vectors. The simplest possible function that possesses a single frequency and nothing else is a sinusoid: cos(nt) or sin(nt). To probe our signal for a particular frequency n, we can use such sinusoids, with the inner product reporting how much of the signal is contained in that frequency. So, these sinusoids can be regarded as our basis vectors. More concretely, consider

⟨cos(nt), cos(mt)⟩ = π∫−π cos(nt) cos(mt) dt,

where we treat cos(nt) as the signal we’re trying to probe and cos(mt) as the basis vector probe. Using the expansion

cos(nt) cos(mt) = 12 (cos((n − m)t) + cos((n + m)t)),

the integrand becomes a sum of cosine functions. If n ≠ m, each of these cosines completes an integer number of oscillations over [−π, π], so the positive and negative contributions cancel exactly, causing the integral to vanish. These two functions are therefore orthogonal to each other, and this tells us that the frequency m is absent in our signal cos(nt), which makes sense when you think about it. If n = m (with n ≥ 1), however, the first term becomes cos(0) = 1, and the integral comes out to π. The one exception is n = m = 0: the integrand is then just 1, and the integral comes out to 2π instead, so the constant probe is a special case worth remembering later. (The full calculations of these cases are left to the reader.) The sines behave identically among themselves, and a sine is always orthogonal to a cosine over the interval, whatever their frequencies. We can see how our inner product function for Hilbert space nicely mirrors the dot product for Euclidean space. So, the sinusoids can be used as basis vectors. (That this family is rich enough to build every finite-energy signal on the interval, in other words that it spans the whole space, is a deep fact that we will also take on faith.)

Although cos(nt) and sin(nt) are two different basis vectors (they are orthogonal), their inner products with a signal,

⟨f, sin(nt)⟩   and   ⟨f, cos(nt)⟩,

would tell us a similar thing: that the frequency n is present in our signal. It is tempting to probe with just one of the pair, but that would be unreliable, as a component that happens to be a pure sine registers as zero against the cosine probe, since the two are orthogonal, and would slip by completely undetected. Both projections, ⟨f, cos(nt)⟩ and ⟨f, sin(nt)⟩, are therefore needed at every frequency. We can neatly bundle these two, however. To do this, we can use the complex exponential,

eint = cos(nt) + i sin(nt),

so that a single projection,

cn = 12π π∫−π f(t)e−int dt,

performs both probings at once. The inner product of complex-valued functions conjugates the second argument, eint = e−int. This is necessary because without conjugation, the inner product of a complex function with itself could yield a negative or even complex number, which would be nonsensical as a squared length. Conjugation guarantees that ⟨f, f⟩ = ∫ |f(t)|2 dt ≥ 0. Expanding the conjugate e−int = cos(nt) − i sin(nt), the projection separates into:

cn = 12 1π π∫−π f(t) cos(nt) dt − i2 1π π∫−π f(t) sin(nt) dt = an − ibn2.

The real part carries the cosine projection an and the imaginary part carries the sine projection bn, each halved. It is as though, at each frequency n, we carve a small ℝ2 slice out of the infinite-dimensional space and project our signal into that plane. The magnitude of the resulting vector in the complex plane,

|cn| = 12 √(an2 + bn2),

combines both readings into half the overall amplitude of the signal at frequency n, regardless of phase. In essence, this magnitude is the strength of the signal at frequency n.

Now that you have a clear plan, you write up a quick little script. There are things that you realise you need to pay attention to: the scaling factors for normalisation and the final complex number magnitudes. You come up with: 

import numpy as np
import csv

t = []
f = []

with open('signal.csv') as file:
    rows = csv.reader(file)
    next(rows)
    for row in rows:
        t.append(float(row[0]))
        f.append(float(row[1]))

t = np.array(t)
f = np.array(f)
N = len(f)

comps = []
mags = []
for n in range(0,21):
    probe = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * n * t)
    if n == 0:
        c = np.dot(f, probe) / N # constant probe, no double counts
    else:
        c = np.dot(f, probe) * 2 / N
    m = np.sqrt(c.real**2 + c.imag**2)
    comps.append(c)
    mags.append(m)
    print(c)

freqs = []
for n in range(0,21):
    if mags[n] > 0.1:
        freqs.append(n)

freqs.sort(reverse=True)
print(freqs)

Some Words from the Author

I first encountered Fourier Transforms when I was reading a paper titled "DEMO: Decoupled Momentum Optimization" from the research lab, Nous Research. In it, the Discrete Cosine Transform was used to compress tensors carrying momentum information before they were shared to different GPUs during the training of neural nets over distributed compute clusters. By reducing the size of the information that had to be transmitted, the authors reduced the bandwidth required to achieve model convergence and allowed for things like geographically distributed compute.

In this paper, the DCT was used to extract and select the top-k frequencies present in the tensor (which was taken to be a signal that could be compressed). Since these top frequencies would contain most of the information in the momentum tensor, the rest of the frequencies could be discarded, and the signal could be reconstructed using just those top frequencies. It was a really clever method with an amazing result. I spent the next two months trying to learn about this transform. I ended up writing a simple implementation of the Discrete Fourier Transform on a toy signal I crafted.

Seeing math on paper turn into actually working code is such a satisfying experience. Some of the components of this story mirror my own thought process as I was learning. Most of the texts that I consumed were really hard to understand at first, and I had to consult multiple sources (lecture notes from Gilbert Strang on OCW, an amazing playlist on YouTube by Professor Brad Osgood and Claude Opus) to help me better understand these ideas. When I wrote this essay, I wanted to write something that I would have loved to have had when I was just starting out. I hope that it does a good job of capturing the joy of discovery and imparting a good intuition for why Fourier transforms are necessary.

I’m currently working on applying the DCT to compressing part of the neural network training process, such that I can reduce the computational requirements to train or run certain networks. Thank you so much for reading my story, and I hope that it taught you something new.
